# Discourse VII: On Freedom from Passion
*(Περὶ ἀπαθείας)*

### 1. The Meaning of Being Beyond Passion

**Interlocutor:** Could it be that just as a pomegranate is termed "seedless," or certain people are described as "neckless" or "chestless," so too men are called "painless" and "fearless[span_0](start_span)[span_1](start_span)"[span_0](end_span)[span_1](end_span)?

**Teles:** Do you want us to follow that line of thought, and say that just as a pomegranate is called seedless and men chestless, so they are termed "blameless" or "free from envy[span_2](start_span)[span_3](start_span)"[span_2](end_span)[span_3](end_span)? 

In those cases, a man is not called "chestless" because he literally lacks ribs, but because his chest is defective; and a fool is called "mindless," not from an absolute lack of mind, but from possessing a flawed mind[span_4](start_span)[span_4](end_span)[span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span). Do you really want to argue that a fearless or painless man simply has broken fear or poor grief[span_6](start_span)[span_6](end_span)[span_7](start_span)[span_7](end_span)? Surely such hair-splitting is completely ridiculous[span_8](start_span)[span_8](end_span)[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span)!

Rather, just as a blameless man is one who stands completely outside of blame, and the unenvious man is outside of envy, so the painless and fearless man stands genuinely beyond pain and terror[span_10](start_span)[span_10](end_span)[span_11](start_span)[span_11](end_span). Only in this state can a person be truly happy—by standing outside of passion and emotional turmoil[span_12](start_span)[span_12](end_span)[span_13](start_span)[span_13](end_span). 

For if a man lives in anguish, grief, and dread, how could he ever be content with life[span_14](start_span)[span_14](end_span)[span_15](start_span)[span_15](end_span)? And if he is not content, how could he be happy[span_16](start_span)[span_16](end_span)[span_17](start_span)[span_17](end_span)? 

Once grief takes hold of him, how can he escape fear, anxiety, anger, and pity[span_18](start_span)[span_18](end_span)[span_19](start_span)[span_19](end_span)? When misfortunes happen, men grieve; when they anticipate them, they tremble in dread; when they see others suffer undeservedly, they are consumed by pity; when people suffer by deliberate choice, they flare up in anger; when they see enemies prosper, they burn with envy; and when rivals stumble, they gloat with malice[span_20](start_span)[span_20](end_span)[span_21](start_span)[span_21](end_span)[span_22](start_span)[span_22](end_span)[span_23](start_span)[span_23](end_span). 

If a man is trapped in grief, how can he be free from passion[span_24](start_span)[span_24](end_span)[span_25](start_span)[span_25](end_span)? And if he is free from no passion, how can he possess peace of mind[span_26](start_span)[span_26](end_span)[span_27](start_span)[span_27](end_span)?

### 2. Death and the Spartan Mothers

The truly serene man must be so fortified that he does not grieve over the death of a friend, or the loss of a child, or even over his own death[span_28](start_span)[span_28](end_span)[span_29](start_span)[span_29](end_span). 

Do you not consider those people cowardly who face their own death with whining and terror[span_30](start_span)[span_30](end_span)[span_31](start_span)[span_31](end_span)? Shouldn't a brave and steadfast man bear his own end with composure, as Socrates did, without complaint or petulance[span_32](start_span)[span_32](end_span)[span_33](start_span)[span_33](end_span)? Why then should you judge someone else's death as more terrible than your own[span_34](start_span)[span_34](end_span)[span_35](start_span)[span_35](end_span)? > *'And you, O mother, drench not your cheek with tears[span_38](start_span)[span_38](end_span)[span_39](start_span)[span_39](end_span)...'*

Yet when a real woman acts on that wisdom, common opinion calls her unfeeling and cruel[span_40](start_span)[span_40](end_span)[span_41](start_span)[span_41](end_span)! 

Everyone professes to admire the Spartan women for their stout hearts[span_42](start_span)[span_42](end_span)[span_43](start_span)[span_43](end_span). When a Spartan mother received word that her son had survived battle by fleeing from the enemy, she wrote to him[span_44](start_span)[span_44](end_span)[span_45](start_span)[span_45](end_span):
> *'A shameful rumor is spreading about you; wipe it out, or never show your face to us again[span_46](start_span)[span_46](end_span)[span_47](start_span)[span_47](end_span)[span_48](start_span)[span_48](end_span)[span_49](start_span)[span_49](end_span)!'*

She did not act like an Athenian mother, who on hearing her boy was safe would cry: *"Bless you, my child, for saving yourself for me[span_52](start_span)[span_52](end_span)[span_53](start_span)[span_53](end_span)!"*

When a herald reported to another Spartan mother that her son had fallen in the front rank, she asked only: *"How did he conduct himself[span_54](start_span)[span_54](end_span)[span_55](start_span)[span_55](end_span)?"*

- *"Good, my child,"* she answered[span_58](start_span)[span_58](end_span)[span_59](start_span)[span_59](end_span). *"For this very purpose I bore you: that you might be a defender and champion of Sparta[span_60](start_span)[span_60](end_span)[span_61](start_span)[span_61](end_span)."*  
She did not weep or scream in despair; hearing that he died valiantly, she gave thanks[span_62](start_span)[span_62](end_span)[span_63](start_span)[span_63](end_span).

And look at that other Spartan mother when her sons fled home from combat[span_64](start_span)[span_64](end_span)[span_65](start_span)[span_65](end_span). When they arrived at her doorstep, she pulled up her garments and demanded: 
> *"Where do you think you are fleeing[span_66](start_span)[span_66](end_span)[span_67](start_span)[span_67](end_span)? Do you intend to crawl back into the womb from which you came[span_68](start_span)[span_68](end_span)[span_69](start_span)[span_69](end_span)?"*

Consider whether any of our women would ever do that[span_70](start_span)[span_70](end_span)[span_71](start_span)[span_71](end_span)! But the Spartans preferred their kin to die with honor, engraving on their tombs[span_74](start_span)[span_74](end_span)[span_75](start_span)[span_75](end_span):

### 3. The Absurdity of Prolonged Grief

How is it not completely irrational and useless to sit weeping and wasting away because a friend has died[span_78](start_span)[span_78](end_span)[span_79](start_span)[span_79](end_span)? 

If you want to appear like a true philosopher among those paralyzed by grief, weep *before* your friend dies—by reminding yourself every day that your companion was born a mortal human being[span_80](start_span)[span_80](end_span)[span_81](start_span)[span_81](end_span)! 

As Stilpon said, neglecting the living on account of the dead is the mark of someone incapable of reasoning[span_82](start_span)[span_82](end_span)[span_83](start_span)[span_83](end_span). A farmer does not act that way: if one tree in his orchard withers and dies, he does not go around cutting down all the rest[span_84](start_span)[span_84](end_span)[span_85](start_span)[span_85](end_span)! Instead, he tends to the surviving trees and nurtures them to make up for the loss[span_86](start_span)[span_86](end_span)[span_87](start_span)[span_87](end_span).

We would never act so foolishly with our own bodies[span_88](start_span)[span_88](end_span)[span_89](start_span)[span_89](end_span). If a man loses one eye, it would be sheer lunacy if he gouged out the remaining one[span_90](start_span)[span_90](end_span)[span_91](start_span)[span_91](end_span); or if he injured one foot, to deliberately cripple the other; or if a tooth fell out, to tear out all the rest[span_92](start_span)[span_92](end_span)[span_93](start_span)[span_93](end_span)! Anyone who behaved like that would be locked away as a madman[span_94](start_span)[span_94](end_span)[span_95](start_span)[span_95](end_span). 

Yet when a wife or son dies, do you think it reasonable to neglect your own life and ruin everything you possess[span_96](start_span)[span_96](end_span)[span_97](start_span)[span_97](end_span)? 

When a neighbor loses a child or a wife, you step forward to console him, urging him to bear it like a man with courage and dignity[span_98](start_span)[span_98](end_span)[span_99](start_span)[span_99](end_span). Yet when the very same circumstance strikes your own house, you indulge in unrestrained hysterics[span_100](start_span)[span_100](end_span)[span_101](start_span)[span_101](end_span)! You advise others to measure their distress, not to view life as unlivable, and to balance apparent evil against real good—matching *"My friend has died"* with *"Yes, but he lived[span_102](start_span)[span_102](end_span)[span_103](start_span)[span_103](end_span)!"* 

Why do you torment yourself over the fact that he is gone, while feeling zero gratitude that he was ever here[span_104](start_span)[span_104](end_span)[span_105](start_span)[span_105](end_span)? You declare yourself miserable because he can no longer share his companionship, but you never count yourself blessed for the years he did[span_106](start_span)[span_106](end_span)[span_107](start_span)[span_107](end_span)!

### 4. Non-Existence and the Steersman's Cry

**Interlocutor:** *"Yes, but he will exist no longer[span_108](start_span)[span_108](end_span)[span_109](start_span)[span_109](end_span)!"*

**Teles:** Well, he did not exist ten thousand years ago, either[span_110](start_span)[span_110](end_span)[span_111](start_span)[span_111](end_span)! You feel no grief over his absence from the past; why torment yourself because he will not exist in the future[span_114](start_span)[span_114](end_span)[span_115](start_span)[span_115](end_span)?

**Interlocutor:** *"Because I am deprived of his companionship[span_116](start_span)[span_116](end_span)[span_117](start_span)[span_117](end_span)!"*

As Socrates said, it remains uncertain whether the man who takes a beautiful bride finds an advantage or a curse, and equally uncertain whether a man gains an advantage from children and friends[span_120](start_span)[span_120](end_span)[span_121](start_span)[span_121](end_span).

How contradictory to urge a friend to embark on military service and fulfill his civic duty—criticizing him as a coward if he refuses—and then weep and lament the moment he sails off[span_126](start_span)[span_126](end_span)[span_127](start_span)[span_127](end_span)!

In that exact same spirit, let the good man say to Fortune:
> *'Do what you will, Fortune; but at all events, you will find me a man, and never a coward[span_130](start_span)[span_130](end_span)[span_131](start_span)[span_131](end_span)!'*
