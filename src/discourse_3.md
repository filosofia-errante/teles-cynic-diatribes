# Discourse III: On Exile
*(Περὶ φυγῆς)*

### 1. The Skills of the Soul Travel With You

To the man who imagines that exile makes people less capable or less intelligent, one could aptly reply with an analogy drawn from the practical arts[span_0](start_span)[span_0](end_span): 

Just as a flute player or an actor does not become worse at his craft simply because he performs in a foreign country, so too an advisor is no worse at offering wise counsel when abroad[span_1](start_span)[span_1](end_span). 

And to anyone who believes that exile is harmful in some other way, let us recall the words of Stilpon[span_2](start_span)[span_2](end_span):

> *"What do you mean?" he asked. "From what kind of goods does exile strip a man[span_3](start_span)[span_3](end_span)? Goods of the soul, goods of the body, or external goods[span_4](start_span)[span_4](end_span)? Does exile strip you of sound reasoning, righteous conduct, or true success[span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span)?"*
> 
> *"Certainly not[span_6](start_span)[span_6](end_span)."*
> 
> *"Does it deprive you of courage, justice, or any other virtue[span_7](start_span)[span_7](end_span)?"*
> 
> *"Not of these either[span_8](start_span)[span_8](end_span)."*
> 
> *"Then does it rob you of any bodily goods[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span)? Cannot a man enjoy good health, physical vigor, sharp eyesight, and keen hearing just as well in a foreign land[span_10](start_span)[span_10](end_span)? Often, aren't they even better maintained abroad than if he had stayed at home[span_11](start_span)[span_11](end_span)?"*
> 
> *"Very true[span_12](start_span)[span_12](end_span)."*
> 
> *"Then surely exile does not rob you of external goods, does it[span_13](start_span)[span_13](end_span)? Have we not seen the fortunes and prominence of countless men actually increase once they were cast into exile[span_14](start_span)[span_14](end_span)?*
> 
> *Was not Phoenix banished from Dolopia by his father Amyntor, only to find refuge in Thessaly[span_15](start_span)[span_15](end_span)?*
> 
> > *'To Peleus I came,*  
> > *And he made me wealthy, assigning many people to my care[span_16](start_span)[span_16](end_span).'*
> 
> *And did not the great Themistocles say to his son in Persian exile[span_17](start_span)[span_17](end_span):*  
> > *'My boy, we would have been ruined had we not been ruined[span_18](start_span)[span_18](end_span)!'*

There is no shortage of such examples[span_19](start_span)[span_19](end_span). Of what true good, then, does exile rob anyone, or of what real evil is it the cause[span_20](start_span)[span_20](end_span)? I cannot see any[span_21](start_span)[span_21](end_span). Rather, it is we ourselves who bury our own lives in obscurity, whether we are driven into exile or stay safely in our homeland[span_22](start_span)[span_22](end_span).

### 2. Kings, Citizens, and the Illusion of Public Office

*"Exiles do not govern,"* people complain; *"they are not trusted with power; they have no right to speak their minds[span_23](start_span)[span_23](end_span)."*

Yet many exiles command garrisons for kings, govern whole provinces, and receive vast gifts and revenues[span_24](start_span)[span_24](end_span). Did not Lycinus, an exile from Italy, hold military command over our own city in the service of King Antigonus[span_25](start_span)[span_25](end_span)? And while we remained right here in our native land, did we not obey the orders Lycinus gave[span_26](start_span)[span_26](end_span)? 

Does not Hippomedon the Spartan currently hold a royal governorship in Thrace under King Ptolemy[span_27](start_span)[span_27](end_span)? And to pass over ancient history and speak of our own contemporaries: are not the Athenians Chremonides and Glaucon seated as royal councilors and prime advisors in Egypt[span_28](start_span)[span_28](end_span)? Was not the former dispatched in command of a great naval fleet, entrusted with enormous treasure, and given absolute authority to direct operations as he saw fit[span_29](start_span)[span_29](end_span)?

*"Yes,"* you say, *"but an exile does not govern in his own native land[span_30](start_span)[span_30](end_span)."*

Neither do women when they remain at home, nor children, nor these young students here, nor men past their physical prime[span_31](start_span)[span_31](end_span). Is this a catastrophe for them[span_32](start_span)[span_32](end_span)? If they were to torment themselves over not ruling the city, wouldn't they be regarded as completely effeminate and unhinged[span_33](start_span)[span_33](end_span)? 

And what real difference is there between holding political office and living as a private citizen[span_34](start_span)[span_34](end_span)? 

You may rule over many men in the prime of life; I rule over a handful of young boys as a teacher; and ultimately, I govern myself[span_35](start_span)[span_35](end_span)! The very same practical wisdom that enables a statesman to govern a multitude in public allows a private man to govern his own household, whether at home or abroad[span_36](start_span)[span_36](end_span). What difference does it make to my well-being if I live as a private citizen rather than an official[span_37](start_span)[span_37](end_span)?

### 3. Changing Ships, Changing Cities

*"But you are barred from entering your own homeland[span_38](start_span)[span_38](end_span)!"*

Well, as a man, I am barred right now from entering the sacred shrine of the Thesmophorion; women are barred from entering the precinct of Enyalius; and all of us are barred from stepping foot inside forbidden sanctuaries[span_39](start_span)[span_39](end_span). If someone were to grieve over being kept out of those places, wouldn't he be acting like a foolish child[span_40](start_span)[span_40](end_span)? 

Sometimes I am barred from entering the local gymnasium; so I simply walk over to the public bathhouse, rub myself down with the same wrestler’s oil, and go on with my exercise[span_41](start_span)[span_41](end_span). 

In the exact same way, having recognized my native land as ground I can no longer walk upon, I pack up and settle elsewhere[span_42](start_span)[span_42](end_span). Just as a traveler can step off one ship onto another and enjoy the exact same favorable voyage, so a man can move from one city to another and enjoy the exact same happiness[span_43](start_span)[span_43](end_span)! 

There is no dishonor or misfortune in this for me, unless I choose to live among vicious men[span_44](start_span)[span_44](end_span). And if I am cast out while being fair and just, is the disgrace mine, or does it belong to those who expelled me[span_45](start_span)[span_45](end_span)? 

Philemon expressed this neatly[span_46](start_span)[span_46](end_span). When he came out of a courtroom having conducted himself with integrity, some bystanders approached him and said: *"How fortunate you have been today, Philemon[span_47](start_span)[span_47](end_span)!"* 

He replied: *"You think that because you are merely watching from the outside; but I know that I have been fortunate because I have remained an honest man throughout[span_48](start_span)[span_48](end_span)."*

### 4. The Judgment of the Crowd

*"What then?"* you ask. *"Isn't it an outrage to be banished by men worse than yourself[span_49](start_span)[span_49](end_span)?"*

*"Would you prefer,"* he replied, *"to be banished by good and upright men[span_50](start_span)[span_50](end_span)? Isn't that what your objection implies[span_51](start_span)[span_51](end_span)? For just and honorable men never banish anyone unfairly or maliciously; if they did, they would cease to be just[span_52](start_span)[span_52](end_span)."*

*"Then isn't it humiliating to be defeated in the voting assemblies by inferior rivals[span_53](start_span)[span_53](end_span)?"*

The humiliation is not yours; it falls squarely on the citizens who cast those votes[span_54](start_span)[span_54](end_span)! If a city bypasses the finest physician and chooses a quack seller of potions to run the public clinic, do you call that a disgrace for the physician, or for the fools who elected the quack[span_55](start_span)[span_55](end_span)?

*"Still, to discover that your native land—for which you sacrificed and labored—is vicious and ungrateful: how can that fail to be a misfortune[span_56](start_span)[span_56](end_span)?"*

How on earth is that a misfortune[span_57](start_span)[span_57](end_span)? Should you not rather count it a stroke of good fortune to learn someone's true character when you were previously blind to it[span_58](start_span)[span_58](end_span)? 

If you discovered that your wife was treacherous and plotting against you, you would be relieved to know it[span_59](start_span)[span_59](end_span). If you found out that your household servant was a thief and a runaway, you would be glad to have uncovered the truth so you could take precautions[span_60](start_span)[span_60](end_span). Yet when you discover that your home city is corrupt and ungrateful, you weep over your 'misfortune' instead of being grateful that your eyes have been opened[span_61](start_span)[span_61](end_span)!

### 5. The Fetish of Native Soil

*"Nevertheless, it seems to me a great privilege to live and die in the land where one was born and bred[span_62](start_span)[span_62](end_span)."*

Would you insist on living in the specific house where you were born and bred, even if the walls were rotten, the roof leaking, and the foundation collapsing around your ears[span_63](start_span)[span_63](end_span)? 

If you had been born aboard a tiny, leaky fishing dinghy and sailed it since childhood, would you refuse to step aboard a seaworthy, twenty-oared galley, preferring to burst your lungs rowing a sinking wreck simply because it was your original craft[span_64](start_span)[span_64](end_span)?

Men mock someone for being an islander from Cythera, Myconos, or tiny Belbina; yet they still claim that living out one's days where one was born is a sacred duty[span_65](start_span)[span_65](end_span)! They admit that most cities are thoroughly corrupt and their citizens impious, yet they treat one's 'native country' as something sacred and comforting in and of itself[span_66](start_span)[span_66](end_span).

They fling the title of 'resident foreigner' (*metic*) as an insult[span_67](start_span)[span_67](end_span):
> *'You metic,*  
> *No native son, yet holding this land in thrall[span_68](start_span)[span_68](end_span)!'*

Yet you revere Cadmus, the founder of Thebes, even though he was a Phoenician foreigner; but you look down on me because I lack local citizenship[span_69](start_span)[span_69](end_span)? You celebrate Heracles as the greatest of heroes, yet consider being a resident foreigner a badge of shame—forgetting that Heracles himself was exiled from Argos and lived out his life as a settler in Thebes[span_70](start_span)[span_70](end_span)! 

The Spartans attach no disgrace to these things[span_71](start_span)[span_71](end_span). If an outsider or the son of a helot submits to their discipline and abides by the laws, they honor him as an equal among their finest citizens[span_72](start_span)[span_72](end_span); but if a man abandons that discipline—even if he is the king's own son—they strip him of rights and cast him among the helots[span_73](start_span)[span_73](end_span).

### 6. The Equal Road to Hades

*"But to be denied burial in one's own ancestral soil—how is that not a disgrace[span_74](start_span)[span_74](end_span)?"*

How can something that has happened to the greatest men in history be a disgrace[span_75](start_span)[span_75](end_span)? And what kind of honor is a public grave, when the vilest politicians receive one every day[span_76](start_span)[span_76](end_span)? 

People praise Socrates for denouncing the Athenians: 
> *"The generals in whom this city took pride lie buried beyond our borders in foreign earth; but the shameful disgraces of our democracy lie honored in public monuments[span_77](start_span)[span_77](end_span)!"*

What real difference does it make whether your corpse is laid to rest abroad or at home[span_78](start_span)[span_78](end_span)? 

An Attic exile gave a sharp retort when someone mocked him, saying: *"You will never even be buried in your native soil; you will rot in the dirt of Megara like a criminal[span_79](start_span)[span_79](end_span)!"* 

The exile replied: *"No, I will be buried in Megarian soil just like the most honorable citizens of Megara[span_80](start_span)[span_80](end_span)! What difference does the dirt make[span_81](start_span)[span_81](end_span)?"*

As Aristippus asked: 
> *"Is not the road to Hades identical and equal in distance from every point on earth[span_82](start_span)[span_82](end_span)?"*

What does it matter if you are not buried at all[span_83](start_span)[span_83](end_span)? 

*"The dread of burial,"* remarked Bion, *"has spawned endless tragic dramas[span_84](start_span)[span_84](end_span)."* Look at Polyneices in the play, wailing to his family:
> *'Bury me, Mother, and you, my sister,*  
> *In my father's land; appease the angry city,*  
> *That I may win at least this much of native earth,*  
> *Though I have lost my home[span_85](start_span)[span_85](end_span)...'*

If you fail to 'win your native earth' and are buried in a foreign country, what changes[span_86](start_span)[span_86](end_span)? Does Charon’s ferry carry men to the underworld only if they set sail from Thebes[span_87](start_span)[span_87](end_span)?

And if your body is left unburied on the battlefield, what harm is there in that[span_88](start_span)[span_88](end_span)? What difference does it make whether you are consumed by flames on a pyre, torn apart by dogs, picked clean by ravens above the ground, or gnawed by worms beneath it[span_89](start_span)[span_89](end_span)[span_90](start_span)[span_90](end_span)?

> *'Close my eyelids with your own gentle hands, Mother[span_91](start_span)[span_91](end_span)...'*

And if your mother does not close your eyelids, and you die with your eyes staring wide open, what agony comes from that[span_92](start_span)[span_92](end_span)? Does anyone close the eyes of sailors who drown at sea, or soldiers who fall in the frenzy of war[span_93](start_span)[span_93](end_span)? 

All these burial anxieties are childish games of the imagination[span_94](start_span)[span_94](end_span). We shudder at the mere thought of touching a dead corpse[span_95](start_span)[span_95](end_span); yet the Egyptians embalm their dead, keep the mummies inside their living rooms as prized treasures, and even pledge them as security for financial loans[span_96](start_span)[span_96](end_span)[span_97](start_span)[span_97](end_span)! 

Their customs are the exact opposite of ours—proving that our terror of the unburied corpse is nothing but habit and empty convention[span_98](start_span)[span_98](end_span).
