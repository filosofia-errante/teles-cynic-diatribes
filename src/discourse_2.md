# Discourse II: On Self-Sufficiency

### 1. Playing the Assigned Role

Just as a good actor must perform skillfully whatever part the playwright assigns, so too must a good man perform whatever role Fortune casts him in[span_0](start_span)[span_0](end_span)[span_1](start_span)[span_1](end_span). 

"For Fortune," says Bion, "like a dramatic poet, sometimes assigns the role of protagonist, sometimes of supporting player; at one moment a king, at another a vagrant[span_2](start_span)[span_2](end_span)[span_3](start_span)[span_3](end_span). Therefore, if you are cast as a supporting player, do not demand the lead; otherwise, you will introduce discord into the drama[span_4](start_span)[span_4](end_span)[span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span)."

"You govern well, and I am governed," he says[span_6](start_span)[span_6](end_span)[span_7](start_span)[span_7](end_span). "You lead many men, while I guide this one youth as tutor; you, being wealthy, give with generosity, while I receive from you with confidence—neither groveling, nor debasing myself, nor whining about my lot[span_8](start_span)[span_8](end_span)[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span). You make good use of great abundance, and I of little[span_10](start_span)[span_10](end_span)[span_11](start_span)[span_11](end_span). For costly luxuries do not nourish us, nor is it possible to use them without harm; but the modest and inexpensive can readily be used with moderation and freedom from conceit[span_12](start_span)[span_12](end_span)[span_13](start_span)[span_13](end_span)."

### 2. Poverty Pleads Her Case

Therefore, as Bion says, if circumstances could find a voice like ours and plead their own case, wouldn't Poverty speak up[span_14](start_span)[span_14](end_span)[span_15](start_span)[span_15](end_span)? 

Just as a household slave, having taken refuge at an altar, defends himself to his master: *"Why are you attacking me? Have I stolen from you? Don't I perform every duty you assign? > *Are not the roads full of wild greens, and the natural springs overflowing with water[span_26](start_span)[span_26](end_span)[span_27](start_span)[span_27](end_span)? Do I not provide as many beds as the earth can span, with fallen leaves for mattresses[span_28](start_span)[span_28](end_span)[span_29](start_span)[span_29](end_span)? Is it impossible to find joy in my company[span_30](start_span)[span_30](end_span)[span_31](start_span)[span_31](end_span)? Do you not see old women eating plain barley porridge and chirping with delight[span_32](start_span)[span_32](end_span)[span_33](start_span)[span_33](end_span)?*

> *Do I not provide you with the most effortless and natural seasoning—hunger itself[span_34](start_span)[span_34](end_span)[span_35](start_span)[span_35](end_span)? > *And do I not offer you shelter free of cost: the public bathhouses in winter, and the sacred colonnades in summer[span_44](start_span)[span_44](end_span)[span_45](start_span)[span_45](end_span)? If Poverty were to say this to you, what could you possibly say in reply[span_48](start_span)[span_48](end_span)[span_49](start_span)[span_49](end_span)? For my part, I think I would be struck dumb[span_50](start_span)[span_50](end_span)[span_51](start_span)[span_51](end_span).

### 3. Taking Circumstances by the Right Handle

Many misguided men shift the blame away from themselves onto external circumstances[span_56](start_span)[span_56](end_span)[span_57](start_span)[span_57](end_span). 

Bion observed that just as handling wild beasts determines whether you get bitten—if you grab a viper by the middle of its body you will be struck, but if you take it firmly by the neck you will suffer no harm—so it is with circumstances[span_58](start_span)[span_58](end_span)[span_59](start_span)[span_59](end_span). Pain arises from how you take hold of them[span_60](start_span)[span_60](end_span)[span_61](start_span)[span_61](end_span). If you lay hold of them as Socrates did, you will feel no distress; but if you take them otherwise, you will torment yourself—tormented not by events themselves, but by your own habits and false assumptions[span_62](start_span)[span_62](end_span)[span_63](start_span)[span_63](end_span)[span_64](start_span)[span_64](end_span)[span_65](start_span)[span_65](end_span).

Therefore, we must not attempt to alter the facts of life, but rather adapt ourselves to meet them, just as sailors do[span_66](start_span)[span_66](end_span)[span_67](start_span)[span_67](end_span). Sailors do not attempt to rearrange the winds and the sea; instead, they prepare themselves to work with whatever arises[span_68](start_span)[span_68](end_span)[span_69](start_span)[span_69](end_span). Clear skies and calm water? They row with the oars[span_70](start_span)[span_70](end_span)[span_71](start_span)[span_71](end_span). A following breeze? They hoist the sails[span_72](start_span)[span_72](end_span)[span_73](start_span)[span_73](end_span). A headwind strikes? They furl the canvas and ride it out[span_74](start_span)[span_74](end_span)[span_75](start_span)[span_75](end_span). 

Look at what you have before you, and use it[span_76](start_span)[span_76](end_span)[span_77](start_span)[span_77](end_span)! 

- You have grown old: do not demand the pursuits of youth[span_78](start_span)[span_78](end_span)[span_79](start_span)[span_79](end_span). 
- You have grown frail: do not try to haul the heavy loads of a strong laborer and break your neck[span_80](start_span)[span_80](end_span)[span_81](start_span)[span_81](end_span). Just as Diogenes, when an aggressive lout was jostling him and twisting his neck when he was indisposed, did not get angry; pointing instead to a stone pillar, he remarked: *"My good fellow, stand over there and shove that[span_82](start_span)[span_82](end_span)[span_83](start_span)[span_83](end_span)!"*
- You have fallen into poverty: do not seek the lifestyle of the rich[span_84](start_span)[span_84](end_span)[span_85](start_span)[span_85](end_span). Just as you adjust your cloak to the weather—throwing it open in mild sunshine, wrapping it tightly against the frost—so you must adjust to your resources: in abundance, loosen up; in scarcity, draw in your folds[span_86](start_span)[span_86](end_span)[span_87](start_span)[span_87](end_span). 

We fail to find contentment in our present lot because we dedicate ourselves to luxury, treat honest physical labor as a catastrophe, and dread death as the absolute worst of evils[span_88](start_span)[span_88](end_span)[span_89](start_span)[span_89](end_span). But if someone learns to look down on pleasure, ceases to resent hard work, regards praise and obscurity with equal balance, and casts off the terror of death, he will be free to do whatever needs doing without distress[span_90](start_span)[span_90](end_span)[span_91](start_span)[span_91](end_span).

### 4. The Market of Luxuries vs. The Market of Necessities

I simply fail to see how circumstances themselves possess anything inherently unbearable, whether it be old age, poverty, or living as a foreigner[span_92](start_span)[span_92](end_span)[span_93](start_span)[span_93](end_span)[span_94](start_span)[span_94](end_span)[span_95](start_span)[span_95](end_span). 

As Xenophon neatly put it: *"If I show you two brothers who inherited identical shares of wealth, one of whom lives in perpetual distress while the other lives in complete ease, isn't it obvious that money is not the cause, but something else[span_96](start_span)[span_96](end_span)[span_97](start_span)[span_97](end_span)?"* 

In the same way, if I show you two old men, two poor men, or two exiles, one of whom lives in cheerfulness and serenity while the other suffers in constant turmoil, isn't it clear that neither old age, nor poverty, nor exile is to blame, but their own attitude[span_98](start_span)[span_98](end_span)[span_99](start_span)[span_99](end_span)?

Consider what Diogenes did to the man who complained that Athens was an excessively expensive city[span_100](start_span)[span_100](end_span)[span_101](start_span)[span_101](end_span):

Diogenes took him by the hand and led him straight to the perfumer's shop and asked the price of a half-pint of Cyprian perfume[span_102](start_span)[span_102](end_span)[span_103](start_span)[span_103](end_span). 
- *"A hundred drachmas!"* said the merchant[span_104](start_span)[span_104](end_span)[span_105](start_span)[span_105](end_span). 
- *"A ruinously expensive city!"* shouted Diogenes[span_106](start_span)[span_106](end_span)[span_107](start_span)[span_107](end_span). 

He then led him to the meat stall and asked the price of pickled pigs' trotters[span_108](start_span)[span_108](end_span)[span_109](start_span)[span_109](end_span). 
- *"Three drachmas!"* 
- *"A ruinously expensive city!"* he exclaimed[span_110](start_span)[span_110](end_span)[span_111](start_span)[span_111](end_span). 

Next, to the fine wool merchant: 
- *"How much for this fleece?"* 
- *"A hundred drachmas!"* 
- *"An expensive city indeed!"* he cried[span_112](start_span)[span_112](end_span)[span_113](start_span)[span_113](end_span).

*"Come over this way,"* said Diogenes, guiding him across to the lupine stall[span_114](start_span)[span_114](end_span)[span_115](start_span)[span_115](end_span). 
- *"How much for a peck of lupine beans[span_116](start_span)[span_116](end_span)[span_117](start_span)[span_117](end_span)?"* 
- *"One copper coin[span_118](start_span)[span_118](end_span)[span_119](start_span)[span_119](end_span)."* 
- *"What an astonishingly cheap city!"* shouted Diogenes[span_120](start_span)[span_120](end_span)[span_121](start_span)[span_121](end_span). 

Then to the dried figs: 
- *"Two coppers[span_122](start_span)[span_122](end_span)[span_123](start_span)[span_123](end_span)."* 
- *"And the myrtle berries?"* 
- *"Two coppers[span_124](start_span)[span_124](end_span)[span_125](start_span)[span_125](end_span)."*

Thus, you see, the city itself is neither inherently cheap nor expensive; rather, if you live luxuriously, it is costly; if you live simply, it is inexpensive[span_128](start_span)[span_128](end_span)[span_129](start_span)[span_129](end_span). ### 5. Facing the End of the Lease

*Interlocutor:* "Still, poverty seems to me to involve something harsh and burdensome; one surely praises a man who bears old age serenely in poverty far more than one who does so with wealth[span_132](start_span)[span_132](end_span)[span_133](start_span)[span_133](end_span)."

**Teles:** What is so harsh or burdensome about poverty[span_134](start_span)[span_134](end_span)[span_135](start_span)[span_135](end_span)? Weren't Crates and Diogenes poor[span_136](start_span)[span_136](end_span)[span_137](start_span)[span_137](end_span)? When debts and scarcity pressed in, Crates said: *"Gather the bean and the pulse, and what goes with them; do this, and you will effortlessly set up a victory trophy over Poverty[span_140](start_span)[span_140](end_span)[span_141](start_span)[span_141](end_span)."*

Why should we praise the man who bears old age with poverty any more than one who does so with riches[span_142](start_span)[span_142](end_span)[span_143](start_span)[span_143](end_span)? Many people manage wealth in old age with sour misery, just as they handle poverty with whining and servility[span_144](start_span)[span_144](end_span)[span_145](start_span)[span_145](end_span). The noble character handles both with dignity; whoever can manage prosperity rightly can handle its reverse just as well[span_146](start_span)[span_146](end_span)[span_147](start_span)[span_147](end_span).

And as long as living in poverty remains viable, one should remain in life; but when it ceases to be viable, one can depart freely, as if leaving a public festival[span_148](start_span)[span_148](end_span)[span_149](start_span)[span_149](end_span). 

As Bion said: 
> *"Just as we are evicted from a rented house when the landlord, receiving no rent, takes away the door, removes the roof tiles, and blocks the well—so too am I evicted from this little body when Nature, my landlady, takes away my eyes, my ears, my hands, and my feet[span_150](start_span)[span_150](end_span)[span_151](start_span)[span_151](end_span)[span_152](start_span)[span_152](end_span)[span_153](start_span)[span_153](end_span). I do not cling to it; but just as I leave a dinner party without complaint, so I take my leave from life[span_154](start_span)[span_154](end_span)[span_155](start_span)[span_155](end_span). When the hour arrives: 'Step aboard the ferryman's skiff[span_156](start_span)[span_156](end_span)[span_157](start_span)[span_157](end_span)!'*
> 
> *Just as a skilled actor plays well the prologue, the middle acts, and the final scene, so the good man lives well through his early years, his prime, and his end[span_158](start_span)[span_158](end_span)[span_159](start_span)[span_159](end_span). And just as I cast off a threadbare cloak when it is worn through, so I do not drag things out or cling desperately to breath; when I can no longer flourish, I take my leave[span_160](start_span)[span_160](end_span)[span_161](start_span)[span_161](end_span)."*

### 6. Socrates: The Master of Playfulness

Look at Socrates[span_162](start_span)[span_162](end_span)[span_163](start_span)[span_163](end_span).
