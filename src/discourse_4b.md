# Discourse IVB: A Comparison of Poverty and Wealth
*(Σύγκρισις πενίας καὶ πλούτου)*

### 1. Wealth as an Obstacle to Philosophy

**Interlocutor:** Poverty prevents a man from practising philosophy, whereas wealth is useful for it[span_136](start_span)[span_136](end_span)[span_137](start_span)[span_137](end_span).

**Teles:** That is completely wrong[span_138](start_span)[span_138](end_span)[span_139](start_span)[span_139](end_span)! How many men do you suppose have been robbed of leisure by wealth, compared to those held back by poverty[span_140](start_span)[span_140](end_span)[span_141](start_span)[span_141](end_span)? Don't you see that the poorest men are usually the ones philosophizing, while the rich are tied up in endless obligations because of their very possessions[span_142](start_span)[span_142](end_span)[span_143](start_span)[span_143](end_span)? 

Theognis spoke well:
> *'Surfeit has destroyed far more men than hunger[span_144](start_span)[span_144](end_span)[span_145](start_span)[span_145](end_span).'*

Where can you show me men blocked from philosophy by poverty in the way they are blocked by riches[span_146](start_span)[span_146](end_span)[span_147](start_span)[span_147](end_span)? Poverty compels men to build endurance, while wealth produces the exact opposite[span_148](start_span)[span_148](end_span)[span_149](start_span)[span_149](end_span). When a person can instantly indulge every desire without effort, he ceases to work or seek deeper truth; having wealth as an accomplice to his vice, he denies himself no indulgence[span_150](start_span)[span_150](end_span)[span_151](start_span)[span_151](end_span). 

The rich are barred from philosophical reflection because they are too busy managing affairs, whereas the poor man, freed from business, turns directly to philosophy[span_152](start_span)[span_152](end_span)[span_153](start_span)[span_153](end_span).

### 2. Crates and the Shoemaker

Zeno related that Crates was once sitting in a shoemaker's workshop, reading aloud Aristotle’s *Protrepticus* (*Exhortation to Philosophy*)[span_154](start_span)[span_154](end_span)[span_155](start_span)[span_155](end_span). 

Aristotle had addressed the work to Themison, king of Cyprus, claiming that no man was better equipped to philosophize than a king—for he possessed vast wealth to finance the pursuit, while retaining the high social standing of royalty[span_156](start_span)[span_156](end_span)[span_157](start_span)[span_157](end_span). 

While Crates was reading this passage, the shoemaker was listening attentively while continuing to stitch leather[span_158](start_span)[span_158](end_span)[span_159](start_span)[span_159](end_span)[span_160](start_span)[span_160](end_span)[span_161](start_span)[span_161](end_span). Crates paused, looked at him, and said: 
> *"Philiscus, I think I ought to write an Exhortation to Philosophy for you; for I see that you have far more genuine advantages for philosophy than the king for whom Aristotle wrote[span_162](start_span)[span_162](end_span)[span_163](start_span)[span_163](end_span)!"*

### 3. Independence and the Burdens of War

Ordinary household slaves manage to feed themselves and still pay a regular fee to their masters; can a free man not even support himself[span_164](start_span)[span_164](end_span)[span_165](start_span)[span_165](end_span)? 

The man without an estate is far freer to enjoy his leisure precisely because he owns nothing to worry over[span_166](start_span)[span_166](end_span)[span_167](start_span)[span_167](end_span). In the present war, for instance, the poor man cares only for himself; the wealthy man is consumed with anxiety over his tenants, his estates, and his goods[span_168](start_span)[span_168](end_span)[span_169](start_span)[span_169](end_span)! 

Sophocles depicted this accurately when he had King Oedipus say:
> *'Your sorrow touches only one soul,*  
> *While I groan for myself, the city, and all of you[span_170](start_span)[span_170](end_span)[span_171](start_span)[span_171](end_span).'*

Yet despite seeing this daily reality, people still consider themselves unfortunate if they are poor[span_172](start_span)[span_172](end_span)[span_173](start_span)[span_173](end_span)!

### 4. True Honour vs. Golden Chains

People say that in the cities, the rich are held in higher honour than the poor[span_174](start_span)[span_174](end_span)[span_175](start_span)[span_175](end_span). 

Those who claim this must never have heard of Aristeides[span_176](start_span)[span_176](end_span)[span_177](start_span)[span_177](end_span). He was the poorest citizen in Athens, yet the most honoured; when the Athenians needed someone to assess tributes on the allied cities, they chose Aristeides, knowing that no man would be more incorruptibly just[span_178](start_span)[span_178](end_span)[span_179](start_span)[span_179](end_span). 

Callias, the richest man in Athens, went out of his way to claim kinship with Aristeides, rather than Aristeides boasting of Callias[span_180](start_span)[span_180](end_span)[span_181](start_span)[span_181](end_span); and Aristeides was far more embarrassed by Callias' lavish wealth than Callias was by Aristeides' honest poverty[span_182](start_span)[span_182](end_span)[span_183](start_span)[span_183](end_span)!

Or look at Lysander the Spartan: who won greater renown or received higher civic honours[span_184](start_span)[span_184](end_span)[span_185](start_span)[span_185](end_span)? Yet when he died, he was so poor that he could not provide dowries for his daughters[span_186](start_span)[span_186](end_span)[span_187](start_span)[span_187](end_span)[span_188](start_span)[span_188](end_span)[span_189](start_span)[span_189](end_span). 

One could name countless others who, though poor, were held in far higher esteem than the wealthy[span_190](start_span)[span_190](end_span)[span_191](start_span)[span_191](end_span). Euripides was right when he praised Eteocles:
> *'A young man in modest poverty,*  
> *Yet crowned with the highest honours in the Argive city[span_192](start_span)[span_192](end_span)[span_193](start_span)[span_193](end_span).'*
