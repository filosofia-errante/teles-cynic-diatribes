# Discourse IVA: A Comparison of Poverty and Wealth

### 1. The Hoarder’s Want

**Interlocutor:** It seems to me that acquiring money frees a person from scarcity and want[span_0](start_span)[span_0](end_span)[span_1](start_span)[span_1](end_span).

**Teles:** How so[span_2](start_span)[span_2](end_span)[span_3](start_span)[span_3](end_span)? Don't you see people who have amassed large fortunes, yet through stinginess and squalor never touch what they own[span_4](start_span)[span_4](end_span)[span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span)? 

Just like Priam, who did not dare sit upon his throne though he had abundance throughout his palace, but sat rolled in filth on the dirt floor—so these hoarders, despite owning vast resources, taste nothing and touch nothing out of pure illiberality[span_6](start_span)[span_6](end_span)[span_7](start_span)[span_7](end_span). The mice and ants eat more of their wealth than they do[span_8](start_span)[span_8](end_span)[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span)! 

They live like Laertes, who retreated into isolation with a lone servant woman, neglecting and starving himself while the suitors devoured his estate[span_10](start_span)[span_10](end_span)[span_11](start_span)[span_11](end_span). Or like Tantalus in the underworld, standing in the pool while branches heavy with fruit hang over his head: whenever the old man strains to drink or reach the fruit, the water vanishes into dry ground and the winds scatter the fruit into the dark clouds[span_12](start_span)[span_12](end_span)[span_13](start_span)[span_13](end_span). In the very same way, the meanness and anxieties of the miser scatter his wine, grain, and produce—not into the clouds, but out to the public markets and taverns for others to enjoy, while he starves in desire[span_14](start_span)[span_14](end_span)[span_15](start_span)[span_15](end_span).

If such a man is invited to dine at someone else’s table, he devours food greedily; but from his own stores, he shares with nobody, trembling in his miserliness[span_16](start_span)[span_16](end_span)[span_17](start_span)[span_17](end_span). If someone else evicted him from his home, he would treat that person as an enemy; yet when he evicts himself from enjoying his own wealth, he never considers himself his own enemy[span_18](start_span)[span_18](end_span)[span_19](start_span)[span_19](end_span)!

The ancients put the matter well: whatever fails to free a man from insatiable craving, pettiness, and conceit will never free him from poverty and want[span_20](start_span)[span_20](end_span)[span_21](start_span)[span_21](end_span). Material gain alters none of these moral defects[span_22](start_span)[span_22](end_span)[span_23](start_span)[span_23](end_span). Just as poverty cannot corrupt the character of a disciplined man who loses his riches, so wealth cannot reform a petty man[span_24](start_span)[span_24](end_span)[span_25](start_span)[span_25](end_span). One could sooner argue that possessing gold changes a person's skin colour, stature, or appearance than that it transforms his inner character[span_26](start_span)[span_26](end_span)[span_27](start_span)[span_27](end_span). As long as he remains insatiable, fearful, and petty, he will live in desperate scarcity[span_28](start_span)[span_28](end_span)[span_29](start_span)[span_29](end_span).

### 2. The Illusion of Ownership

**Interlocutor:** But how can men who hold vast possessions be in scarcity[span_30](start_span)[span_30](end_span)[span_31](start_span)[span_31](end_span)?

**Teles:** In the exact same way, says Bion, that money-changers hold gold[span_32](start_span)[span_32](end_span)[span_33](start_span)[span_33](end_span)! The money passing through their counters does not belong to them, and neither does the miser's wealth belong to him[span_34](start_span)[span_34](end_span)[span_35](start_span)[span_35](end_span). Even if someone hands you gold to hold, possessing something you cannot use is identical to not possessing it at all[span_36](start_span)[span_36](end_span)[span_37](start_span)[span_37](end_span). 

What difference is there between having nothing and possessing an eye like the Graeae—who kept a single shared eye locked away in storage, while they stumbled blindly into ditches, mud pits, and fences, until Perseus snatched it away entirely[span_38](start_span)[span_38](end_span)[span_39](start_span)[span_39](end_span)?

What difference is there between having no food, and having food that taboo forbids you from touching—like fish and doves to those who revere them, dog meat to an Egyptian, or human flesh to a Greek[span_40](start_span)[span_40](end_span)[span_41](start_span)[span_41](end_span)? The man with food he cannot eat is just as starved as the man with an empty cupboard[span_42](start_span)[span_42](end_span)[span_43](start_span)[span_43](end_span). What benefit is wealth if squalor and dread keep you from touching a single drachma[span_44](start_span)[span_44](end_span)[span_45](start_span)[span_45](end_span)?

The ancients observed that some men possess *riches* (*chremata*—things meant for use), while others possess only *caching-stores* (*ktemata*)[span_46](start_span)[span_46](end_span)[span_47](start_span)[span_47](end_span). The former use their resources for life; the latter merely guard them, neither spending on themselves nor giving to others[span_48](start_span)[span_48](end_span)[span_49](start_span)[span_49](end_span). Just as you cannot touch property that an autocrat has sealed under royal wax, so the miser's own petty fear puts a seal upon his wealth, keeping him in constant poverty and deprivation[span_50](start_span)[span_50](end_span)[span_51](start_span)[span_51](end_span).

### 3. Crates and the Frugal Life

When a student asked Crates, *"What will I gain if I become a philosopher?"*, Crates answered[span_52](start_span)[span_52](end_span)[span_53](start_span)[span_53](end_span):
> *"You will be able to open your wallet easily, dipping your hand in to give freely and generously—not hesitating, trembling, and making excuses like a man whose hands are paralyzed[span_54](start_span)[span_54](end_span)[span_55](start_span)[span_55](end_span). When your bag is full, you will look upon it without vanity; when you see it empty, you will feel no sorrow[span_56](start_span)[span_56](end_span)[span_57](start_span)[span_57](end_span). When you choose to use money, you will do so without stress; and when you have none, you will not yearn for it[span_58](start_span)[span_58](end_span)[span_59](start_span)[span_59](end_span). You will live content with what is present, unconcerned with what is absent, and untroubled by whatever comes your way[span_60](start_span)[span_60](end_span)[span_61](start_span)[span_61](end_span)."*

If you wish to free someone from poverty and want, do not seek more money for him[span_62](start_span)[span_62](end_span)[span_63](start_span)[span_63](end_span). 

As Bion noted, that is like trying to cure a man suffering from dropsy by giving him rivers and gushing springs instead of treating the disease[span_64](start_span)[span_64](end_span)[span_65](start_span)[span_65](end_span). The patient will burst from drinking before his thirst is quenched[span_66](start_span)[span_66](end_span)[span_67](start_span)[span_67](end_span). Similarly, an insatiable, vain, and superstitious mind can never be filled[span_68](start_span)[span_68](end_span)[span_69](start_span)[span_69](end_span). If you want your son freed from want, do not send him to King Ptolemy's court to chase money—he will return with an inflated ego and accomplish nothing[span_70](start_span)[span_70](end_span)[span_71](start_span)[span_71](end_span). Send him to Crates, who knows how to turn greedy, extravagant men into free and unpretentious minds[span_72](start_span)[span_72](end_span)[span_73](start_span)[span_73](end_span)!

### 4. Metrocles’ Transformation

Metrocles himself recalled that when he studied at the Lyceum under Theophrastus and Xenocrates, despite receiving generous funds from home, he lived in constant dread of starving and felt perpetually broke[span_74](start_span)[span_74](end_span)[span_75](start_span)[span_75](end_span). But once he crossed over to Crates, he had enough to feed others even when nothing was sent from home[span_76](start_span)[span_76](end_span)[span_77](start_span)[span_77](end_span)!

Under the academics, he was forced by social expectation to wear fine, unstitched sandals, expensive cloaks, maintain an entourage of servants, keep a grand house, provide fine wheat bread for shared dinners, secure delicacies, serve sweet wine, and host lavish parties—for all that vanity was considered 'genteel' among them[span_78](start_span)[span_78](end_span)[span_79](start_span)[span_79](end_span). 

When he turned to Crates, all that pretense was discarded[span_80](start_span)[span_80](end_span)[span_81](start_span)[span_81](end_span). He adopted simple habits, content with a coarse threadbare cloak, barley-cakes, and wild greens—neither longing for his past luxury nor fretting over his present simplicity[span_82](start_span)[span_82](end_span)[span_83](start_span)[span_83](end_span).

Against the cold, we scramble for thicker garments; Metrocles simply doubled over his single cloak, effectively wearing two[span_84](start_span)[span_84](end_span)[span_85](start_span)[span_85](end_span). When he needed an oil rub, he used the public scrapings at the bathhouse[span_86](start_span)[span_86](end_span)[span_87](start_span)[span_87](end_span). When he needed hot food, he would step by the bronze furnace, fry some sprats, pour a dash of oil over them, and eat[span_88](start_span)[span_88](end_span)[span_89](start_span)[span_89](end_span). He slept in temple colonnades in summer and public bathhouses in winter, lacking nothing and desiring no servants[span_90](start_span)[span_90](end_span)[span_91](start_span)[span_91](end_span). 

As Diogenes remarked: 
> *"How strange it would be if the slave Manes can live without Diogenes, but Diogenes cannot manage without Manes[span_92](start_span)[span_92](end_span)[span_93](start_span)[span_93](end_span)!"*

### 5. The Insatiable Ladder

If you pile riches onto an arrogant, extravagant, superstitious soul, you cure nothing[span_94](start_span)[span_94](end_span)[span_95](start_span)[span_95](end_span). As Philemon said:
> *'You will acquire another fortune, not another character[span_96](start_span)[span_96](end_span)[span_97](start_span)[span_97](end_span).'*

As long as that flawed character remains, no amount will suffice[span_98](start_span)[span_98](end_span)[span_99](start_span)[span_99](end_span). For the wicked possess an insatiable mind[span_100](start_span)[span_100](end_span)[span_101](start_span)[span_101](end_span):
- As a child, he aches to become a youth[span_102](start_span)[span_102](end_span)[span_103](start_span)[span_103](end_span).
- As a youth, he is in a hurry to cast aside the youthful cloak and enter adulthood[span_104](start_span)[span_104](end_span)[span_105](start_span)[span_105](end_span).

- When old age arrives, he weeps for childhood: *"Youth was dear to me, but old age is heavier than Mount Aetna[span_108](start_span)[span_108](end_span)[span_109](start_span)[span_109](end_span)[span_110](start_span)[span_110](end_span)[span_111](start_span)[span_111](end_span)!"*

Look at the slave[span_112](start_span)[span_112](end_span)[span_113](start_span)[span_113](end_span): 
- *"If only I obtain my freedom,"* he says, *"I will have everything[span_114](start_span)[span_114](end_span)[span_115](start_span)[span_115](end_span)."* 
- He gains his freedom; at once he wants to buy a slave[span_116](start_span)[span_116](end_span)[span_117](start_span)[span_117](end_span). 
- He buys one; immediately he needs a second—*"for one swallow does not make a spring[span_118](start_span)[span_118](end_span)[span_119](start_span)[span_119](end_span)."* 
- Then he must have a house, then an estate, then citizenship in Athens, then a magistracy, then a crown, then immortality like Alexander—and if he achieved that, he would want to become Zeus[span_120](start_span)[span_120](end_span)[span_121](start_span)[span_121](end_span)!

How is such a man not destitute[span_122](start_span)[span_122](end_span)[span_123](start_span)[span_123](end_span)? What amount of money could ever quench these cravings[span_124](start_span)[span_124](end_span)[span_125](start_span)[span_125](end_span)? Kings themselves, despite ruling broad realms and collecting massive tributes, live in such scarcity that they rob graves, pillage temples, and banish innocent citizens for confiscations[span_126](start_span)[span_126](end_span)[span_127](start_span)[span_127](end_span). Whoever rises above all these desires lives in true plenty and boundless wealth[span_130](start_span)[span_130](end_span)[span_131](start_span)[span_131](end_span). As Crates proclaimed:
> *'You know not how much power lies in a beggar's pouch,*  
> *A peck of lupines, and complete freedom from care[span_132](start_span)[span_132](end_span)[span_133](start_span)[span_133](end_span).'*

It is a truly great and noble achievement to need nothing beyond a wallet, lupines, wild greens, and clear water—unfettered by flattery, submissive to no one[span_134](start_span)[span_134](end_span)[span_135](start_span)[span_135](end_span).
